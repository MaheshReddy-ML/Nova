# Photo art direction for small-business sites

Real photographs are the strongest trust signal a small business site has. A visitor decides in a few seconds whether
the place is real, welcoming and worth the trip, and they decide mostly from pictures. This playbook covers how to pick,
order, crop and describe photos for a one-page site, and what to do when the business has too few.

## 1. Inventory before layout

- Count usable photos first, then choose the layout. 0 photos: typographic design with illustration panels. 1-3: hero +
  story image, text-led cards. 4-7: photo-led offering cards. 8+: add a gallery.
- Usable means: at least 1000 px on the long side, in focus, not a logo, flyer, screenshot, menu scan, collage or
  text-heavy social graphic, and not obviously another business's picture.
- Prefer the business's own photos over anything else, even when stock looks more polished. Authenticity beats polish
  for local businesses: their actual space, people and work.

## 2. Choosing the hero

- The hero shows the experience the customer is buying, not the building or the logo: hands on clay, a plated dish,
  the stylist at work, the room at golden hour.
- Look for one clear subject, natural light, depth (foreground/background separation) and calm space where text or a
  badge can sit without covering faces.
- Portrait or 4:5 crops suit split heroes; 3:2 or wider suits full-bleed heroes. Never use a 1200x630 social card or a
  photo with baked-in text as the hero.
- Faces: if people appear, they look engaged in the activity, not posing at the camera. Avoid identifiable children
  unless the business published that image itself.

## 3. Sequencing down the page

1. Hero: the core experience (the "why come").
2. Offerings: one photo per offering, each showing that exact thing. A pottery class card shows a wheel, not a cafe.
3. Story: the people or the place (the "who").
4. Gallery: variety: detail shots, wide shots, finished work, atmosphere. Alternate close and wide; avoid three
   similar shots in a row.
- Keep a consistent colour temperature across the page; do not mix a cold blue photo next to warm amber ones in one row.
- Never show the same photo twice on one page.

## 4. Cropping

- Use object-fit: cover with an intentional focal point; keep heads and hands inside the safe area (centre 70%).
- Card images share one aspect ratio (4:3 or 1:1) so the grid reads as a set.
- Do not upscale small images; a sharp small image beats a blurry large one.

## 5. Alt text

- Describe what the photo shows and why it matters on this page, in 5-15 words: "Hands shaping a bowl on a pottery
  wheel", not "image", "photo 1" or the file name.
- Do not start with "Image of". Do not stuff keywords. Decorative duplicates get empty alt text.

## 6. Representative stock photos

- Only when the business has fewer than about four usable photos, and only with licenses that allow commercial use
  and modification (CC0, CC BY, CC BY-SA, public domain). Record title, creator, license and source, credit them on
  the page, and tell the client they are placeholders to replace with their own.
- Search for the specific activity and material, not the category: "hands pottery wheel clay", "resin pour
  coasters", "bento cake piping", "acoustic open mic small stage"; not "art", "business" or "happy people".
- Avoid stock clichés: handshakes, people pointing at laptops, staged laughter at salads, thumbs up, generic
  skylines, light bulbs, puzzle pieces, and anything with watermarks or obvious foreign signage that contradicts the
  location.
- Prefer candid, documentary-style images with natural light and real materials. Skip images where the subject is
  tiny, heavily filtered or obviously illustration/3D render.

## 7. Plan fields

When a design system asks for photo subjects, list 3-6 short concrete subjects: first the hero experience, then one per
offering in the same order. Each is 2-5 words describing a visible scene (people + action + material).
