# Scientific Critical Thinking

One sentence: Evaluates scientific claims and evidence quality by assessing methodology, biases, statistical validity, and evidence grading frameworks to identify flaws and support rigorous analysis.

## When to use

- Evaluating research methodology and experimental design
- Assessing statistical validity and evidence quality
- Identifying biases and confounding in studies
- Reviewing scientific claims and conclusions
- Conducting systematic reviews or meta-analyses
- Applying GRADE or Cochrane risk of bias assessments
- Providing critical analysis of research papers

## Workflow

1. **Fix the question and unit.** Record population, intervention/exposure, comparator, outcome, time point, effect measure, and target setting. Distinguish descriptive, predictive, and causal claims. Identify the independent experimental/sampling unit.
2. **Extract evidence before judging.** Locate the numerical result, uncertainty, denominators, protocol/registration, analysis plan, and relevant supplementary material. Keep missing reporting separate from evidence that a procedure was not performed.
3. **Assess design and analysis.** Check selection, confounding, measurement, attrition, multiplicity, dependence, and model assumptions. Good fit or optimizer convergence does not establish a uniquely identified parameter or a causal effect.
4. **Choose the right appraisal framework.** Reporting completeness, risk of bias, and certainty of a body of evidence answer different questions. Record the exact tool version and the result being assessed; do not turn checklist counts into a quality score.
5. **Synthesize with scope intact.** Examine independent replication, overlapping samples, missing evidence, and applicability. Apply GRADE per outcome/comparison when appropriate, with explicit domain reasons, rather than grading a whole paper by its design label.
6. **Write a traceable critique.** For each concern give the source location, observation, consequence for the claim, uncertainty, and a feasible remedy. Separate supported conclusions from assumptions and from clinical/policy recommendations.

## Quality bar (checklist)

- Question and unit clearly fixed (population, intervention, comparator, outcome, time point, effect measure)
- Evidence extracted numerically before appraisal (results, uncertainty, denominators, protocol, analysis plan)
- Design and analysis assessed for selection, confounding, measurement, attrition, multiplicity, dependence, and model assumptions
- Appropriate framework selected (GRADE, Cochrane RoB 2, ROBINS-I, QUADAS-3) with exact version recorded
- GRADE applied per outcome/comparison with explicit domain reasons, not whole-paper grading
- Critique structured: summary, strengths, concerns (critical/important/minor), recommendations, overall assessment
- Specific terminology used: named biases, fallacies, methodological issues with references to established standards
- Uncertainty acknowledged with conditional assessments and clarifying questions when needed
- Supported conclusions separated from assumptions and policy recommendations

## Pitfalls

- Turning checklist counts into a single quality score rather than assessing reporting completeness, risk of bias, and certainty separately
- Assigning one blanket risk-of-bias score to an entire paper when different outcomes can have different bias judgments
- Confusing statistical significance with practical importance or causal effect
- Mixing domains or algorithms from different bias tools (e.g., combining ROBINS-I 2016 and V2 draft domains)
- Using QUADAS-2 instead of current QUADAS-3 (v1.2) for diagnostic accuracy appraisal
- Grading a whole paper by its design label rather than applying GRADE per outcome/comparison
- Including unpublished sensitive details in AI schematic generation prompts without appropriate transmission consideration
- Presenting teaching examples from references as empirical findings or validated patient-specific advice
